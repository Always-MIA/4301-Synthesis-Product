# Where to Go From Here

## Finding the Right Starting Point

Mental-health resources can sometimes feel overwhelming, especially when you are not sure what kind of help you need.

Instead of looking for one universal solution, it can help to start with what you are experiencing and then identify the type of support that may apply.

## "I'm Exhausted All the Time."

If fatigue or sleep disruption is becoming a regular problem, start by looking at resources related to:

- Sleep and recovery
- Fatigue management
- Scheduling
- Workplace resources
- Employee Assistance Programs

If fatigue is affecting your ability to function safely or consistently, consider discussing the situation with an appropriate healthcare or workplace professional.

## "Work Is Constantly Stressing Me Out."

Consider looking into:

- Employee Assistance Programs
- Peer-support resources
- Workplace or union resources
- Professional counseling
- Stress-management resources

It can also be useful to identify what part of the work environment is creating the most stress rather than treating "work stress" as one single problem.

## "I Feel Completely Burned Out."

Burnout can involve prolonged workplace stress, exhaustion, and feeling disconnected from work.

Possible starting points include:

- Professional mental-health support
- EAP services
- Peer support
- Conversations with appropriate workplace resources
- Resources focused on sleep and recovery

## "Something Really Difficult Happened."

After a serious or potentially traumatic event, available support may include:

- Critical-incident programs
- Peer-support programs
- EAP services
- Professional mental-health care
- Personal support systems

Different people respond differently to difficult events, so there is no single correct timeline for seeking support.

## "I Don't Know What Resources My Airline Offers."

Start with your employer's employee resources or internal support information.

Depending on the airline, you may also have access to:

- EAP
- Occupational health resources
- Peer-support programs
- Union resources
- Critical-incident support

Remember that resources vary between airlines and organizations.

## "I'm Thinking About Becoming a Flight Attendant."

Learning about the realities of the job before entering the industry can help you develop realistic expectations.

Look beyond the travel benefits and destinations and learn about:

- Irregular schedules
- Sleep disruption
- Fatigue
- Passenger interactions
- Workplace stress
- Recovery
- Mental-health resources

The goal is not to discourage someone from entering aviation. It is to provide a more complete picture of the work.

## Start With What You Need

There is no single way to approach mental health and well-being.

The most useful starting point depends on the situation.

If you are looking for general information, begin with educational resources. If you are experiencing ongoing stress or burnout, consider workplace or professional support. If you have experienced a serious event, look into critical-incident and professional resources.

If you are experiencing a mental-health emergency or believe you may be in immediate danger, contact emergency services or an appropriate crisis resource.

## Final Thought

Being a flight attendant means taking care of other people in situations where safety and professionalism matter.

Taking care of yourself matters too.

Understanding the relationship between occupational stress, fatigue, burnout, psychological health, trauma, and support is one step toward making mental health a more visible part of the aviation conversation.
